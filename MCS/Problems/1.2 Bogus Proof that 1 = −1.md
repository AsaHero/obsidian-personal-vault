## Problem

The following "proof" concludes that $1 = -1$:

$$ 1 = \sqrt{1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{-1},\sqrt{-1} = \left(\sqrt{-1}\right)^2 = -1 $$

**(a)** Identify precisely where the error is and explain it.

**(b)** Prove that if $1 = -1$, then $2 = 1$.

**(c)** Every positive real $r$ has two square roots, one positive and one negative. By convention, $\sqrt{r}$ means the **positive** one. Using familiar properties of real multiplication, prove that for positive $r$ and $s$: $$\sqrt{rs} = \sqrt{r},\sqrt{s}$$

---

## Solution

### (a) The error

The error is the step $$\sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{-1},\sqrt{-1}$$

The rule $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a},\sqrt{b}$ holds only when $a \ge 0$ and $b \ge 0$. Here $a = b = -1$, so the rule does not apply. Over the reals, $\sqrt{-1}$ is not even defined, so the step is unjustified.

> [!note] Over the complex numbers Even with $i = \sqrt{-1}$, the rule fails: $\sqrt{-1}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = i \cdot i = -1$, but $\sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{1} = 1$.

> [!warning] Common mistake You cannot "fix" the step by replacing $\sqrt{-1}$ with $-\sqrt{1}$. We have $-\sqrt{1} = -1$ and $(-1)^2 = 1 \ne -1$, so $-\sqrt{1}$ is **not** a square root of $-1$.

### (b) If $1 = -1$, then $2 = 1$

Assume $1 = -1$. Add $3$ to both sides: $$4 = 2$$ Divide both sides by $2$: $$2 = 1 \qquad \blacksquare$$

### (c) $\sqrt{rs} = \sqrt{r},\sqrt{s}$ for $r, s > 0$

**Proof.**

1. By convention, $\sqrt{r} > 0$ and $\sqrt{s} > 0$. The product of two positive reals is positive, so $\sqrt{r},\sqrt{s} > 0$.
2. Using commutativity and associativity of multiplication: $$\left(\sqrt{r},\sqrt{s}\right)^2 = \left(\sqrt{r}\right)^2\left(\sqrt{s}\right)^2 = rs$$
3. So $\sqrt{r},\sqrt{s}$ is a **positive** number whose square is $rs$. By the convention, that number is exactly $\sqrt{rs}$. $\blacksquare$

> [!tip] Connection to (a) Step 1 is where positivity is essential. For $r = s = -1$, the factors $\sqrt{r}, \sqrt{s}$ aren't positive reals, so the proof breaks. That is exactly the bug in the bogus proof.
