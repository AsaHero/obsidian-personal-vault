## Problem

It's a fact that the Arithmetic Mean is at least as large as the Geometric Mean: $$\frac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}$$ for all nonnegative real numbers $a$ and $b$. But there's something objectionable about the following proof of this fact. **What's the objection, and how would you fix it?**

**Bogus proof.** $$ \begin{aligned} \frac{a+b}{2} &\overset{?}{\ge} \sqrt{ab}, &&\text{so} \ a+b &\overset{?}{\ge} 2\sqrt{ab}, &&\text{so} \ a^2 + 2ab + b^2 &\overset{?}{\ge} 4ab, &&\text{so} \ a^2 - 2ab + b^2 &\overset{?}{\ge} 0, &&\text{so} \ (a-b)^2 &\ge 0 &&\text{which we know is true.} \end{aligned} $$ The last statement is true because $a-b$ is a real number, and the square of a real number is never negative. This "proves" the claim.

---

## Solution

### The objection

The proof **starts from the claim** and derives a true statement. That only shows $$\text{claim} \implies \text{true statement},$$ which proves nothing. A false claim can also imply a true statement: $$1 = -1 \implies 1^2 = (-1)^2 \implies 1 = 1 ;✅$$

> [!warning] Common mistake Working backwards from the goal is fine as **scratchwork**, but the final proof must go **forward** from known facts to the claim, with every step justified in that direction.

### The fix: reverse the chain

**Proof.** Let $a, b \ge 0$.

1. $(a-b)^2 \ge 0$, since the square of a real number is never negative.
2. Expanding, $a^2 - 2ab + b^2 \ge 0$.
3. Adding $4ab$ to both sides gives $$a^2 + 2ab + b^2 \ge 4ab, \quad\text{that is,}\quad (a+b)^2 \ge \left(2\sqrt{ab}\right)^2.$$
4. Both $a+b$ and $2\sqrt{ab}$ are **nonnegative** because $a, b \ge 0$. For nonnegative $x, y$, $x^2 \ge y^2$ implies $x \ge y$. Hence $$a + b \ge 2\sqrt{ab}.$$
5. Dividing by $2$: $$\frac{a+b}{2} \ge \sqrt{ab}. \qquad \blacksquare$$

> [!tip] Where the hypothesis is used Step 4 is the only step that isn't automatically reversible. Taking square roots of an inequality requires both sides to be nonnegative. For example, $(-3)^2 \ge 2^2$ but $-3 < 2$. This is exactly where $a, b \ge 0$ is needed.

## Takeaway

- Showing $P \implies \text{True}$ does **not** prove $P$.
- To prove $P$, derive it **from** known truths.
- When reversing a chain, check that each step is valid in the new direction, especially squaring, taking roots, or dividing.
